How Expats Open Bank Accounts Abroad Without Proof of Address

2026-10-24 · Nomad Toolkit

You can open a bank account abroad without a traditional proof of address by using alternatives such as a bank statement from another institution, a tenancy agreement, an employer or university letter, a utility bill in someone else's name plus a confirmation letter, or by choosing a bank that accepts digital nomad and expat documentation. Some banks also accept a self-declaration of address, a local SIM or mobile contract, or a notarized statement. The right route depends on the country and the bank, but almost every expat can find a workable path with the right documents prepared in advance.

Why Banks Ask for Proof of Address at All

Banks are legally required to verify who you are and where you live under anti-money-laundering (AML) and "know your customer" (KYC) rules. Proof of address is not about prying into your life — it confirms you're a real, traceable person and gives the bank a way to reach you. Understanding this helps, because it tells you what you're trying to satisfy: a credible, verifiable link between you and an address.

The good news: regulators care about *verification*, not about one specific document. That's why alternatives are often accepted.

The Problem New Arrivals Face

Most expats hit the same wall: you need a local address to open a bank account, but you need a bank account to rent a place or sign a utility contract. This "chicken-and-egg" problem is common in countries like Germany, Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands, where registration or a local bill is often requested.

The solution is to break the loop with whatever document you *can* get first — and to sequence your admin tasks deliberately.

Accepted Alternatives to a Traditional Proof of Address

Exact rules vary by bank and country, but these are widely recognized substitutes. Many expats find that presenting two or three together is stronger than relying on one.

  • Bank statement from your home or previous bank — often the most universally accepted document. Ask your existing bank for a recent statement showing your name and address.
  • Tenancy agreement or rental contract — even a short-term or Airbnb-style contract can work at some banks.
  • Employer letter — a signed letter confirming your employment and address.
  • University or school enrollment letter — useful for students.
  • Utility or phone bill — a local mobile contract or internet bill in your name.
  • Government correspondence — a tax letter, residence permit application receipt, or registration confirmation.
  • Affidavit or self-declaration of address — some banks accept a sworn statement, sometimes notarized.
  • Letter from your landlord — confirming you live at the address, often with a copy of their ID.

Step-by-Step: Opening an Account Without Standard Proof of Address

1. Decide what "address" you'll use. A hotel, a friend's place, a coworking space, or a short-term rental can sometimes serve as your contact address. Be honest — never invent an address, as this can void your account later. 2. Shortlist banks that are expat-friendly. Digital banks and neobanks (such as those aimed at nomads and internationals) tend to be more flexible than traditional high-street banks. Search "[country] expat bank account" and read recent reviews, since policies change. 3. Contact the bank before you visit. Email or call and ask directly: "What documents do you accept as proof of address for a newly arrived expat?" Get it in writing if possible. 4. Gather a document bundle. Bring your passport, visa or residence permit (if you have one), a home-country bank statement, a tenancy or landlord letter, and an employer or enrollment letter. More evidence beats less. 5. Get documents translated and, if needed, notarized. Many countries require certified translations into the local language. Budget time for this. 6. Consider a self-declaration. Ask whether the bank accepts a sworn statement of address (often called an *affidavit* or *Meldebescheinigung*-style document, depending on the country). 7. Open a digital or multi-currency account as a stopgap. Services like Wise, Revolut, or N26 (availability varies by country) can bridge the gap while you sort local paperwork. 8. Update your address later. Once you have a permanent place, give the bank your new proof of address to keep your account fully compliant.

Country-Specific Tips

  • Germany: An *Anmeldung* (registration) is usually expected, but some banks accept a landlord confirmation while you wait for an appointment.
  • Spain: The *empadronamiento* (municipal registration) is the standard document; a rental contract plus passport can sometimes work at digital banks.
  • Portugal: A *NIF* (tax number) plus a rental contract or utility bill is often enough.
  • Netherlands: A *BSN* and registration letter are typically required; short-term accommodation letters can help in the interim.

Always confirm current requirements locally, as rules and bank policies shift frequently.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using a fake or borrowed address. This can get your account frozen and harm future applications.
  • Bringing only one document. Prepare a bundle.
  • Ignoring translation requirements. Certified translations take time.
  • Assuming all branches apply the same rules. Staff interpretation varies — try another branch if you're refused.
  • Forgetting to update your address. Keep the bank informed once you're settled.
