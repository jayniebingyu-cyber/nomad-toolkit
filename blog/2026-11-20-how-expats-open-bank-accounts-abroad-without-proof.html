How Expats Open Bank Accounts Abroad Without Proof of Address

2026-11-20 · Nomad Toolkit

You can open a bank account abroad without a traditional proof of address by using alternative documents—such as a landlord letter, a local mobile phone bill, a bank statement from your home country, or a notarised declaration of your residential address. Many banks and digital challengers also accept an in-person verification visit, a temporary accommodation confirmation, or an employer/sponsor letter in place of a utility bill. The key is knowing which document each institution will accept before you apply, because requirements vary widely by country and by bank.

Why Banks Ask for Proof of Address at All

Banks are legally required to verify your identity and residential address under anti-money-laundering (AML) and "know your customer" (KYC) rules. The address check exists to confirm you're a real person living where you say you live—not to make your life difficult. Understanding this helps, because it means the requirement is usually about verification, not about the specific document. If you can prove your address another way, many institutions will accept it.

The friction typically arises because newly arrived expats don't yet have the classic documents banks expect: a utility bill in their name, a local tax letter, or a tenancy agreement registered with local authorities. Those can take weeks or months to arrive.

Documents That Often Work Instead

The following alternatives are commonly accepted, though you should always confirm with the specific bank first:

  • Landlord or letting agent letter confirming your address and tenancy dates, ideally on letterhead and signed
  • A registered tenancy or rental contract (in some countries this alone is sufficient)
  • Local mobile phone bill in your name (easier to obtain quickly than utilities)
  • Home-country bank or credit card statement showing your overseas address, if the bank accepts foreign documents
  • Employer or relocation sponsor letter stating your residential address
  • Temporary accommodation confirmation from a hotel, serviced apartment, or Airbnb host (works with some digital banks)
  • A local address registration certificate (e.g., the *Anmeldung* in Germany, *residence certificate* in parts of Asia and Latin America)
  • Notarised self-declaration of address, accepted by a minority of institutions

Choose the Right Type of Bank First

Your choice of institution matters as much as your paperwork.

Digital and challenger banks (especially those built for expats and remote workers) tend to have the most flexible onboarding and often accept a passport plus a selfie and an address you can evidence in more creative ways. The trade-off is that some offer limited local services or no physical branches.

International banks with expat divisions are used to customers who've just moved and often have dedicated onboarding teams. They may accept employer letters or a home-country statement.

Local high-street banks are usually the strictest, but they're also where you'll need to be for local salary deposits, direct debits, and certain government services. If you must use one, visit a branch in person—a face-to-face meeting with a human often resolves document gaps that an online form cannot.

Step-by-Step: Opening an Account Without a Utility Bill

1. Confirm your exact address situation. Are you in permanent housing, temporary accommodation, or staying with friends? This determines which documents you can realistically produce. 2. Shortlist banks that fit your needs. Prioritise institutions known to serve expats or newcomers in your country. Check expat forums and local community groups for recent experiences—requirements change. 3. Call or check the bank's onboarding page before applying. Ask directly: "What can I use as proof of address if I don't have a utility bill?" Get the answer in writing if you can. 4. Assemble a document bundle. Bring more than you think you need: passport, visa or residence permit, landlord letter, tenancy contract, mobile bill, and a home-country statement. 5. Get documents translated and certified if required. Many countries require sworn translations of foreign-language documents; budget time and cost for this. 6. Book an in-person appointment where possible. Branch staff can often verify your address through a site visit, a local registration record, or by escalating to compliance. 7. Consider a digital bank as a bridge. Open an expat-friendly online account to receive salary and pay bills while your local paperwork catches up, then open a local account later. 8. Register your address locally as soon as you can. Once you have the official registration or first utility bill, add it to your file—many banks ask you to update KYC details within a set period anyway.

Practical Tips That Save Time

  • Apply for a local SIM and phone contract early. A mobile bill in your name is one of the fastest proofs of address to obtain.
  • Ask your landlord for a signed letter on day one. It costs them nothing and solves a lot.
  • Keep everything consistent. The address on every document should match exactly—including formatting, apartment numbers, and postcodes. Mismatches trigger manual reviews.
  • Be honest about temporary addresses. Banks generally prefer a declared temporary address over a false permanent one; misrepresentation can get an account frozen.
  • Use a relocation agent or employer HR contact if you have one. They often have template letters banks already recognise.
  • Expect a second verification step. Even if onboarding succeeds, some banks request updated proof of address within 30–90 days.

When You're Stuck: Escalation Options

If a bank rejects your documents, ask to speak to the compliance or onboarding team rather than the front desk. Request the specific policy in writing, and ask what alternative evidence they would accept. In some countries, a local address registration office, notary, or expat community organisation can issue a recognised confirmation. Switching to a different institution is often faster than fighting a rigid process.
