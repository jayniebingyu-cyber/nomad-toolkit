How Expats Open Bank Accounts Abroad Without Proof of Address

2026-11-11 · Nomad Toolkit

You can often open a bank account abroad without a traditional utility bill by using alternative proof-of-address documents—such as a tenancy agreement, a bank statement from another institution, a government letter, or an employer confirmation—or by choosing banks and digital providers that accept alternative verification. The exact options depend on the country and the bank's anti-money-laundering rules, but most expats can satisfy requirements with the right combination of documents and a little planning.

Why Banks Ask for Proof of Address at All

Banks aren't being difficult for the sake of it. Under anti-money-laundering (AML) and "know your customer" (KYC) regulations, financial institutions in most countries must verify both who you are and where you live. Proof of address is part of that second check.

The catch for new expats is obvious: you often need a local address to open an account, but you may need an account to secure a lease, utility contract, or phone line—the very documents banks usually request. This chicken-and-egg problem is common, and there are legitimate ways around it.

Documents That Often Work Instead of a Utility Bill

Before assuming you're stuck, check what your target bank actually accepts. Many institutions list several acceptable documents, and a utility bill is only one option. Commonly accepted alternatives include:

  • Tenancy or rental agreement signed by you and the landlord
  • A bank statement from your existing home-country bank (showing your name and address)
  • Government correspondence such as a tax letter, residence permit, or national ID registration
  • Employer letter confirming your address and employment
  • Property purchase documents if you've bought a home
  • Mobile phone or internet contract in your name
  • Insurance policy documents showing your address

Some banks accept a declaration of address or a sworn statement, particularly when combined with other ID. Ask specifically—frontline staff don't always know the full list, so request the compliance team's document requirements in writing.

Choose the Right Institution

Not all banks treat expats the same way. Your choice of provider matters as much as your paperwork.

Expat-focused and digital banks are often more flexible. Neobanks and international account providers frequently verify address through alternative means, such as a video call, a selfie with a document, or acceptance of foreign statements. Wise, Revolut, N26, and similar providers have varying country availability and requirements, so check eligibility for your location.

Local banks may be stricter but sometimes accept in-person verification, a landlord's confirmation, or a residence registration document.

International banks with a presence in both your home and host country can sometimes open accounts using your existing relationship and home-country address.

A practical tip: open an account before you leave if possible, or shortly after arrival while you still have home-country documents, then update your address later.

Step-by-Step: Opening an Account Without a Local Utility Bill

1. Confirm your residency status. Your visa or residence permit often doubles as address proof or unlocks other documents. 2. Register your address officially if the country requires it (e.g., town hall registration, residence card, or police registration). This generates a government document banks trust. 3. List every document you already have that shows your name and an address—home-country bank statements, tax letters, insurance, tenancy agreements. 4. Shortlist two or three banks and download or request their KYC document lists before applying. 5. Contact the bank directly and ask what they accept for expats without a local utility bill. Get it in writing if you can. 6. Request an employer or landlord letter confirming your address if you're renting or working locally. 7. Apply online where possible, uploading the strongest combination of documents. 8. Prepare for a video or in-person verification—have your passport and documents ready. 9. Update your address with the bank once you have a utility bill or formal registration.

Practical Tips That Save Time

  • Keep documents current. Statements older than three months are often rejected.
  • Match names exactly. A middle name missing from one document can trigger a rejection.
  • Translate and certify documents if the bank requires it—many accept certified translations.
  • Ask about "simplified due diligence" for lower-risk accounts; some banks offer lighter verification for basic accounts.
  • Use a virtual mailbox cautiously. Some banks accept it, others reject it outright—confirm first.
  • Don't guess. A rejected application can slow future attempts, so verify requirements upfront.

When You Genuinely Can't Provide Proof

If every route is blocked, consider these fallbacks:

  • Joint accounts with a partner who has local proof of address
  • Accounts with digital-first providers that verify differently
  • Credit unions or community banks that may be more flexible
  • Waiting until your first utility bill or registration document arrives, then applying

Many expats find that a combination of official registration plus one supporting letter is enough, even without a utility bill.
